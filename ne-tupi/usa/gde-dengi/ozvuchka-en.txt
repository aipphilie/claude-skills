=== COLD OPEN ===
[tired] The night before payday. You open your bank app. [pause] Zero.
[pause] And you've gotten raises. More than once.
You used to make less and hit zero. Now you make more... and hit the exact same zero.
[sarcastic] Seriously, what for?
[pause] [firm] Stop. This is where you're being dumb.
You don't run out of money because you have too little. Every raise leaks out through a specific hole. And today we're going to find all four.
And at the end, there's one rule that makes sure your next raise actually stays with you.
[pause] So where's the money?

=== 1. IT'S NOT PRICES ===
The first thing you want to say is: everything got more expensive. And that's true, prices go up. And if you can't even cover groceries and rent, that's a different conversation and a different video. This one is for people whose paycheck went up, but the zero stayed.
[pause] But let's be honest. Think about what changed for you over the last couple of raises.
A newer phone. Delivery instead of the grocery store. Uber instead of the bus when you don't feel like it. Five subscriptions now. And coffee has to be the "good" coffee.
Not one of these things costs your whole raise. But together, they eat it down to the last cent.
[curious] And the weird part: you didn't even notice. Nobody ever decided, "I'll spend my raise on Ubers." It just dissolved.
It's called lifestyle inflation. Prices go up for everyone. But your lifestyle goes up by exactly the size of your raise.
That's why the zero never goes away. It just moves into a bigger apartment.
[pause] But why do we do this, if nothing actually changes in the end?

=== 2. HOLE NUMBER ONE: YOU GET USED TO IT ===
Remember the day you got your raise? [excited] Amazing! For a week, you feel richer.
A month later, it's just your salary. Three months later, it feels like it's not enough again.
That's not you being greedy. Psychologists call it hedonic adaptation, or the hedonic treadmill. The term goes back to 1971, to psychologists Brickman and Campbell.
Your brain gets used to any upgrade fast and turns it into the new normal.
That's why a new phone makes you happy for a couple of weeks, and then it's just a phone. But the monthly payment stays.
[pause] The joy is gone, the expense is still there. And it's like that with every purchase.
You run faster, but the treadmill under you speeds up too.
[pause] Though on a desert island, it wouldn't speed up like that. Other people are the ones turning up the speed.

=== 3. HOLE NUMBER TWO: THE NEIGHBORS ===
You don't spend money in a vacuum. You see what your friends drive, where your coworkers go on vacation, what everyone's posting.
So your normal isn't "what I need." It's "what everyone around me has."
There's a study that's a little unsettling. Economists Agarwal, Mikhed, and Scholnick looked at data from one Canadian province: who won the lottery, and who filed for bankruptcy.
[pause] And it turned out: the bigger a neighbor's lottery win, the more often the people living next to them went bankrupt.
The neighbor's money fell from the sky. And the people around them start trying to keep up: a nicer car, a remodel... and they end up in debt.
[sarcastic] Great deal.
You do the same thing, just smaller. A coworker flies to Hawaii, and suddenly your staycation feels like a failure.
[pause] But if you don't have the money to keep up, your brain finds a way to buy it right now anyway. And that's where the sneakiest hole begins.

=== 4. HOLE NUMBER THREE: PAYING DOESN'T HURT ===
When you pay cash, you watch the money leave your hand. It stings a little.
When you tap your phone, it doesn't hurt at all. Beep, done.
In 2001, at MIT, researchers ran an auction for basketball tickets. Some people were told they could only pay cash. Others could only pay by card.
[pause] The people paying by card bid noticeably more for the exact same tickets. By some accounts, almost twice as much.
Same tickets. The only difference was how they paid.
[curious] Thought you were buying a thing? Nope. You're buying a thing without the pain.
And "buy now, pay later" is even sneakier. "Just four easy payments" sounds like nothing. Except you've already got six of those "nothings."
Credit is a deal with future you: "He'll pay for it. He'll be richer by then."
[pause] And future you gets a raise... and hands it to the bank to pay for past you.
[playful] Here's the catch: your raise is gone before it even shows up.
[pause] That's three holes so far. But the best part is still coming. There's a fourth one, and it's the big one. More money leaks through it than anywhere else, and you think of it as a reward.

=== 5. HOLE NUMBER FOUR: "I DESERVE IT" ===
Rough week: you order takeout. Finished a project: new sneakers. Got a raise: gotta celebrate.
Every purchase sounds reasonable. You really are tired. You really did earn it.
But notice this: as your paycheck grows, so does the number of reasons to reward yourself.
The more you work for money, the more you spend to recover from that work. It turns into a loop: you work so you can buy things. You buy things so you can put up with work.
[softly] I'm not saying "never treat yourself." Treat yourself. But ask how many of those rewards you'll even remember a week from now.
[pause] So, four holes. Habit. The neighbors. Painless payments. And "I deserve it."
Now look at what they have in common. Not one of them is about what you need. All four are about how you feel. And how other people see you.
[pause] Which brings us to the real answer to "where's the money?"

=== 6. WHERE THE MONEY WENT ===
[slowly] The money didn't disappear. You used it to buy the feeling of having more money.
[pause] That's it.
You got a raise, and you immediately spent it on living like someone with more money. Instead of actually becoming that person.
Morgan Housel writes in "The Psychology of Money": wealth is what you don't see. The car you didn't buy. The vacation on a credit card you didn't take. Money that just sits there quietly.
And that's not just a nice line. Back in the nineties, Thomas Stanley and William Danko studied American millionaires for their book "The Millionaire Next Door." Many of them lived modestly: an ordinary house, an inexpensive car, no showing off.
Meanwhile, people with similar incomes, but with new cars and big houses, often had almost nothing saved.
That's why, from the outside, rich people often look poorer, and poor people look richer. You can see what's been spent. Wealth is invisible.
[excited] So here's the twist: to get richer, you have to stop looking richer.
[pause] But how, if all four holes run on autopilot? Willpower? No. The first rough week will eat your willpower alive.

=== 7. THE HALF RULE ===
You need your raise to disappear before you get used to it. Just not at the store. Into your own account.
Economists Richard Thaler, a future Nobel Prize winner, and Shlomo Benartzi created a program called "Save More Tomorrow."
People weren't asked to save more right now, because that hurts. Instead, they were asked to agree in advance: when the next raise comes, part of it automatically goes into savings.
[pause] Most people said yes. And after about three years, they were saving almost four times as much as when they started.
Why does it work? You're not giving up money you already have. You're giving up money you haven't gotten used to yet. And that doesn't hurt.
[firm] So the rule is simple. Split your next raise in half. Before it even arrives.
Half goes to your life, enjoy it. Half goes into a separate account with an automatic transfer on payday.
[pause] Not after. Not "if there's anything left." On payday.
[playful] Wait, that's allowed? It is.
And the first thing that account becomes is your emergency fund. Without one, you're working out of fear: scared to quit, scared to push back, holding on to any job. An emergency fund is the right to say "no."
[softly] And yes, this isn't investment advice. It's a way to make sure the money has somewhere to come from in the first place.

=== OUTRO ===
So. Money doesn't vanish. It gets eaten by habit, the neighbors, painless payments, and "I deserve it." And you use it to buy the feeling of being richer.
Your plan for today: open your bank app and set up an automatic transfer on payday. Even twenty-five dollars. And when your next raise comes, add half of it to that transfer.
And tell me in the comments which of the four holes is the biggest one for you.
[pause] And next time you open your bank app the night before payday, it won't say zero.
[firm] That's it. Don't be dumb.
